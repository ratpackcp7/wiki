# Learnings from Conducting ~1,000 Interviews at Amazon

**Source:** [The Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/learnings-from-conducting-1000-interviews)
**Expert:** Steve Huynh (Former Principal Engineer & Bar Raiser at Amazon)

## 1. The Core Thesis: Technical Skill is Only the "Ante"
After 17 years and ~1,000 interviews, Steve Huynh observes that candidates rarely fail due to a lack of technical skill. They fail because of how they present themselves and their inability to demonstrate "fit."

> "Technical skills are the ante. They get you into the game. But they’re not what wins you the hand." — *Amazon Bar Raiser Trainer*

## 2. Major Learnings from the Bar Raiser Perspective

### Learning #1: The Preparation Imbalance
Most candidates spend 95% of their time on LeetCode and 5% on behavioral prep. This is a mistake because technical prep has diminishing returns, while behavioral prep has exponential returns.
*   **The Risk:** You cannot "reason" your way through a behavioral question like "Tell me about a time you failed." Without a prepared story, you will likely "ramble and stumble."
*   **Actionable Advice:** Reallocate your time. If you have 80 hours to prep, spend 10 of those hours specifically on story preparation.

### Learning #2: Delivery is as Important as the Story
A great accomplishment can be ruined by poor communication.
*   **The "Ramble and Stumble":** Candidates often figure out the story as they go, losing the interviewer in unnecessary context.
*   **The "Authenticity" Trap:** Many feel rehearsing is "fake." In reality, the "real" you is the one who communicates clearly under pressure.
*   **Actionable Advice:** Record yourself answering "Tell me about yourself" and "Why do you want to work here?" Watch the recording, identify filler words, and iterate until you sound like someone you’d want to work with.

### Learning #3: The Interview is an Audition, Not an Exam
Interviewers aren't looking for a "correct" answer from a rubric; they are trying to visualize working with you.
*   **The Goal:** Can they trust your judgment in a crisis? Can they picture you in a design review?
*   **The Mistake:** Giving "perfect" answers where everything went right. This makes you a "black box" to the interviewer.
*   **Actionable Advice:** Share the "rough edges." Explain the hard parts and the close calls. Honesty about your thought process is more valuable than a polished, flawless story.

## 3. Understanding "Fit" and "Level"

### Role and Company Fit
Companies look for signals that your working style matches their environment:
*   **Independence vs. Collaboration:** Do you run with problems solo or seek group buy-in?
*   **Speed vs. Thoroughness:** Do you ship MVPs quickly (Startup style) or prioritize careful validation (Healthcare/Finance style)?
*   **Direct vs. Diplomatic:** Do you value radical candor or maintaining harmony?

### The Four Dimensions of Leveling
Companies use these four metrics to determine if you are Junior, Senior, Staff, or Principal:

1.  **Scope:** How many people are affected by your work? (One person -> One team -> Multiple teams -> Entire Organization).
2.  **Contribution:** What did *you* specifically do? (Executing tasks -> Owning solutions -> Leading initiatives -> Creating organizational capabilities).
3.  **Impact:** What changed for the better? (Personal productivity -> Team effectiveness -> Business metrics/Strategic change).
4.  **Difficulty:** How complex were the trade-offs? (Straightforward patterns -> Competing requirements -> Multi-system architectural implications -> Novel organizational problems).

## 4. Researching the Company (The "Insider" Edge)
Most candidates do zero research. Standing out requires "intelligence gathering":

*   **The Recruiter:** Treat them as an ally. Ask: *"What competencies matter most for this role?"* or *"What are the company's current challenges?"*
*   **Public Artifacts:** 
    *   **Engineering Blogs:** See what problems they celebrate.
    *   **Postmortems:** Do they value learning from failure or blame-shifting?
    *   **Job Postings:** Look for repeated keywords (e.g., "fast-paced" vs. "compliance").
*   **Current Employees:** Ask specific questions like: *"When someone gets promoted here, what did they do to earn it?"* or *"How does the team make decisions during disagreements?"*

## 5. Summary Table: Leveling Examples

| Level | Scope | Contribution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Entry** | Personal tasks | Implementing solutions designed by others. |
| **Senior** | Entire team + adjacent teams | Driving technical decisions; mentoring through complex problems. |
| **Staff** | 2+ teams; broader division | Defining technical approaches adopted by multiple teams. |
| **Principal** | Large organization; business strategy | Transforming how the organization approaches its hardest challenges. |
