# The Privilege of Picking Problems

**Source**: [https://web.navan.dev/posts/2026-04-28-the-priviege-of-picking-problems.html](https://web.navan.dev/posts/2026-04-28-the-priviege-of-picking-problems.html)

---

## Core Framing: The Mustard Metaphor
- Anecdote: A close friend’s dad believed *"you can never get the perfect amount of mustard on your pretzel. It is always too much or too little."*
- Author initially dismissed this as a joke, but realized *picking* (mustard, life problems) is far harder than complaining about existing options.

---

## The Rails of Early Life vs. "Freedom"
- Most people follow structured life "rails" with obvious next steps: high school → college → job → promotion → harder problems → better title. These are not always pleasant, but remove the need to set your own objectives. You can *"complain about the train while still being grateful that someone else had laid the track."*
- "Freedom" arrives when the rails end: no rubric, institution, or clean next step. You must choose your own constraints and objective function, which is far harder for people used to optimizing against external goals.
- Title credit: Author adopted the title *The Privilege of Picking Problems* after discovering September C. Fawkes wrote a [similar piece in 2016](https://www.septembercfawkes.com/2016/09/the-privilege-of-picking-your-problems.html). Kept the title regardless, noting *"Some titles are too accurate to surrender."* Recommends reading Fawkes’ work, noting they write from different life stages but share the core thesis: picking problems is a privilege *and* a trapdoor.

## On Happiness and Life Priorities
- When asked "Are you happy?", author’s answer: *"I’m not unhappy."* (describes this as precise, not evasive)
- Rejects optimizing for happiness: pleasure is easy to hack, numbness even easier. Maximizing happiness would mean *"lower the resolution, dull the senses, chemically sand down the edges of the world"* – a life not worth defending post-dopamine.
- Guiding principle: *"If I am going to burn cognition anyway, I would prefer not to spend it making the world worse in a more scalable way."*
- Core non-negotiables (not side quests, but the point of life): friends, books, movies, cold beer, silly software experiments, adrenaline, late-night conversations, 8 hours of sleep, problems that are *"just hard enough to be annoying."*

## Work Patterns and Problem Selection Pitfalls
- Author is most alive at the hard center of a problem: when it resists, constraints conflict, no one knows if the approach will work, the problem becomes a *"wall"* not a task.
- Interest leaks once the core idea is cracked; can finish implementation, distribution, maintenance, etc. but *finishing ≠ caring* (caring is the scarce resource). Has shipped full end-to-end projects to prove disinterest is not avoidance.
- Critique of vague *"I like solving problems"* (common among engineers): useless without specifying *which* problems. Key pitfalls author has fallen into:
  - Tempted by elegant problems that don't matter, clever systems that only produce more clever systems: *"I have watched whole rooms of smart people build machinery for machinery and then act confused when someone asks what any of it touches."*
  - Seduction of developer tools/leverage: *"A machine that builds a machine is a beautiful thing"* but the chain of leverage must hit the real world, not terminate in *"better abstractions for abstractions."*
  - Rejects attention economy work: will not spend cognition increasing screen time, stickier feeds, sharper ads, or content matched to user weaknesses (*"already enough smart people making the rectangle harder to escape"*).
  - Loves pure math/theory, but rejects spending life in a *"game whose main consequence is that I got better at playing the game"* – dangerous because it is appealing.
- *Note: This is not a universal judgment.* Author acknowledges the world needs people building dev tools, working on theory, or optimizing systems they personally find corrosive – they are only defining their own fit, not a universal rule.

## Defining a Worthy Problem
### What to Avoid (Negative Space)
- No engagement traps
- No abstraction towers that never touch reality
- No *"beautiful private games"* that feel brilliant while the world stays unchanged

### What to Pursue (Positive Space)
- *"A problem with a pulse"*: real humans (not slide-deck abstractions) at the end of the work, whose lives are materially better if the solution works. *"Stakes that breathe."*
- Perfect problems are usually over-modeled, over-funded, over-explained, or irrelevant. Worthy problems are messy: involve logistics, boring constraints, changing minds, slow institutions, fake budgets, crooked incentives, and *"rude reality."* Author explicitly wants *"reality in the loop."*
